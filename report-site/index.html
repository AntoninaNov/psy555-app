Why we ran it

A diagnostic for curriculum design — at scale

Universities decide how courses connect to each other once, then teach the same structure for years. GPA tells the registrar who passed. It doesn't tell anyone which ideas actually link up in students' heads — or where the programme silently rewards memorisation over comprehension.

So we built a one-hour computerised concept-mapping protocol and ran it on a small cohort first. Twelve students. Exploratory analysis — no formal hypothesis testing on N this small. The point of the pilot was simple: does the instrument produce a usable signal? If yes, the same protocol scales to 300+ students next year and becomes a quantitative curriculum-design tool that reaches what GPA never could.

The protocol

What each session looked like

Each participant ran the same five-block protocol on the study app (≈ 60 minutes per session). The full session traces are in the public JSON data.

A

Cluster

Group the 14 ILOs into 4 thematic clusters; pick a "central" ILO in each cluster.

B

Map

Connect any two ILOs you see related. Weight every edge w ∈ {1, 2, 3} by strength.

C

Self-rate

Rate your own competence on each ILO on a Likert 1–7 scale.

D

Pathfinding

Given two ILOs, choose the shortest weighted path on your own graph (3–4 trials).

E

Perturbation

One ILO is removed. Pick a structural replacement among the remaining nodes (3–4 trials).

Methodology

From session traces to numbers — eight steps

The pipeline below is implemented in figures.py. Each card is one self-contained transformation.

1 · Collect

Data collection

One cohort, KSE Psychology 3rd-year, April 2026. Computerised protocol logs every interaction (clicks, weights, decision times) into structured JSON.

2 · Filter

Sample formation

Nenrolled = 12. Drop S2 (zero edges, incomplete). Analytical N = 11. Pathfinding sub-sample = 2 due to a logged session-launcher misconfiguration.

3 · Code

Outcome variables

Reduce each session to 9 metrics: density, WGD, avgWeight, edgeWeightSD, avgRating, sdRating, bridgeAcc, perturbTime, homophily.

4 · Operationalise

Predictors

GPA from transcript (100-pt). Graph predictors (density, WGD, edgeWeightSD) computed from the participant's adjacency matrix.

5 · Probe

Exploratory probes

Eight directional probes informed by theory (Geary 2008; Dunning & Kruger 1999). Spearman ρ̂S with permutation null, n = 9 999 (seed 42). No formal hypothesis testing at N = 11.

6 · Diagnose

Quality checks

For every probe report ρ̂, pperm, and leave-one-out Δρ. At N = 11 only |ρ| ≳ 0.62 reaches α = .05 — effect sizes are the export.

7 · Interpret

Status classes

Three: direction noted, opposite sign, not resolvable (variance of one input collapsed below the threshold needed to see the contrast).

8 · Caveat

Limitations

Eight categorical limits propagated through every claim — see the Limits section.

Probes — GPA & the ceiling

GPA tracked grades, not graph structure

We ran eight directional probes — four involving GPA, four involving graph-internal structure. The headline pattern is simple: GPA has weak, mostly noisy associations with every concept-graph metric we measured. And four of the eight probes can't be resolved at all in this sample because nine students saturated the graph at density = 1.000.

⚠ Why everyone connected everything

Saturation is a rational response to an under-constrained task: with no edge budget and no penalty for spurious links, students minimise the risk of omitting a "right" connection by drawing them all. The artefact is itself a finding — and the design fix for the next iteration: cap |E|, force a ranking instead of categorical 1–3 weights, score with a precision penalty.

The plain-English reading of P1–P4: students with higher GPAs do not draw measurably more integrated, more hierarchical, or more weight-differentiated concept maps than students with lower GPAs. If GPA tracked the structural quality of subject knowledge, one or more of these four probes should have produced a large, signed effect. None did. That is the central exploratory result of the pilot.

Finding 2 — what survived

Three patterns in the residual variance

Even with the ceiling, three effects emerged from the off-density variables — two of them large, one universal. None was pre-specified at this level of operationalisation; we report them as exploratory, hypothesis-generating for the next study.

A. Higher integration → lower self-rating (the Socratic effect)

Across all eleven participants, weighted graph density correlates negatively with mean self-rated competence: ρ = −0.69, pperm = .023. The effect persists with the Dunning–Kruger outlier removed (ρ = −0.58, N = 10). Students whose maps look more integrated assign themselves lower competence — the textbook Socratic pattern (Flavell, 1979; Eva & Regehr, 2005).

B. More confident → slower under perturbation

Mean self-rating positively predicts mean perturbation time: ρ = +0.77, pperm = .008. Confident students take longer per replacement choice — not shorter. The most natural reading is task investment: the people who rate themselves higher think more carefully when the task confronts them with a structural choice.

C. All eleven participants weight within-cluster ties higher

Without exception, every participant assigned higher mean weights to within-cluster edges than to between-cluster edges. Δ ranges from 0.20 (S6) to 0.90 (S5). Even S7, with only 17 edges, shows Δ = 0.36. The unanimity matters: it confirms that the prior thematic clustering of the 14 ILOs is recovered as a semantic distinction by participants without an explicit prompt — useful evidence for curriculum-cluster validity at the next iteration.

Finding 3 — single case

S7: a textbook Dunning–Kruger profile across nine indicators

One participant (S7, GPA = 93) deviates from the rest of the sample on nine indicators in a coherent direction — peak self-evaluation co-occurs with minimum demonstrated structural competence. Nine simultaneous deviations from the median in the predicted direction is a strong signal even at single-case scope.

Why N = 12 is enough — for now

What this protocol becomes at 300+ students

The pilot was never about settling H1–H6. It was about answering: does the instrument produce a usable signal? Three diagnostic answers — yes, with one major fix.

1. Add an edge budget — stop measuring compliance.

Cap |E| at, say, 30 of the 91 possible. Force the participant to choose. The information content of the resulting graph rises sharply because the prior "everything connects" is no longer cheap.

2. Replace categorical weights with a forced ranking.

Instead of w ∈ {1, 2, 3}, ask the participant to rank their drawn edges from strongest to weakest. edgeWeightSD gets back its differentiating power.

3. Score perturbation and pathfinding with precision-weighted accuracy.

Penalise spurious connections instead of only rewarding present ones. With the homophily pattern (Finding 2C) already established at N = 11, the cluster structure is a free ground truth at scale.

Run with these three changes on the next cohort (anticipated N ≈ 300 across the undergraduate Psychology programme), and the same protocol becomes a quantitative curriculum-design diagnostic: which ILOs cluster in students' minds, which edges are load-bearing, which participants need scaffolding, and where the programme silently encourages compliance over comprehension.

Limits

Eight caveats — read every result through these

1. N = 11

Critical |ρ| at α = .05 is ≈ 0.62. Effects below that are visible but not significant.

2. No multiple-comparisons correction

13 tests reported, no Bonferroni. Effect sizes are the export, not p-values.

3. Density ceiling

Var(density) ≈ 0.06; nine values exactly at 1.000. Four hypotheses untestable as a result.

4. No external knowledge criterion

GPA proxies academic success, not subject competence. Self-rating is not a knowledge measure.

5. Cross-sectional

One snapshot per participant. Test–retest reliability of the metrics is unknown.

6. Pathfinding lost

Block ran for 2/11 participants due to a session-launcher bug. H4 is "not tested", not null.

7. Single cohort

One programme, one institution, one year group. Generalisation is not warranted.

8. Exploratory ≠ confirmatory

Findings 2A–2C and the S7 single case are hypothesis-generating, not established.
