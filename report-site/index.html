What GPA Misses

Chess masters don't remember more pieces — they see them in deeper patterns (Chase & Simon, 1973). GPA averages outputs; it never sees the structure underneath. So we tried to look directly.

Theoretical background

From cognitive maps to concept graphs

Spatial-cognition research since Tolman (1948) has converged on a representational rather than associative account of how organisms encode environments. The empirical case rests on novel behaviour — shortcuts and detours an animal has never been reinforced for — which a chained stimulus–response account cannot generate. Tolman labelled the underlying representation a cognitive map; the term was inferential at the time, but a neural substrate was subsequently identified. Recordings from the rat hippocampus (O'Keefe & Nadel, 1978) isolated place cells whose firing rates encode the animal's position in its environment, providing the first single-unit correlate of a Tolmanian map.

The mapping system extends beyond the hippocampus proper. Hafting, Fyhn, Molden, Moser, and Moser (2005) reported a second cell type in the medial entorhinal cortex — grid cells — whose firing fields tile the environment in a regular hexagonal lattice. The discovery established a metric and coordinate basis for spatial representation; the work was recognised by the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine awarded jointly to John O'Keefe, May-Britt Moser, and Edvard Moser. Of relevance to the present study, Constantinescu, O'Reilly, and Behrens (2016) demonstrated that the same grid-like code is recruited when human participants traverse abstract conceptual dimensions in a non-spatial task, providing direct evidence that the cognitive-map machinery is not modality- specific. We take this as licensing the working assumption that students' representations of programme learning outcomes (PLOs) admit the same graph- theoretic treatment as physical environments.

The structural properties of large biological and informational networks have themselves been characterised: scale-free degree distributions (Barabási & Albert, 1999), small-world routing efficiency (Latora & Marchiori, 2001), and a generalised dependence of behavioural performance on network topology rather than on node count alone. Within cognitive psychology this position has a parallel literature: Chase and Simon (1973) showed that chess expertise produces no gain on randomised boards but a large gain on meaningful positions, locating the advantage in chunking rather than memory capacity; Chi, Feltovich, and Glaser (1981) extended the result to physics, demonstrating that experts categorise problems by underlying principle while novices categorise by surface features. Miller (1956) provides the upper bound on independent chunks in working memory; cognitive load theory (Sweller, 1988) and dual-system models (Kahneman, 2011) describe the failure modes when that limit is exceeded.

A complementary line of work places this question in evolutionary perspective. Geary (2008) distinguishes biologically primary cognitive abilities, which develop without explicit instruction, from biologically secondary abilities such as formal academic content, which are acquired only under sustained external pressure and are vulnerable to evolutionary mismatch when the pressure rewards surface compliance over deep relational reasoning. The wider Darwinian-mismatch framework (Nesse & Williams, 1994) treats this kind of incentive misalignment as a generic source of dysfunction across institutions; in the academic case the predicted symptom is graphs that look correct without being structurally informative.

From this literature we derive a single working assumption: a learner's grasp of a curriculum is more accurately characterised by the topology of the induced concept graph than by an aggregate of exam outputs. A grade-point average marginalises over that topology and is informative about the marginal density of assessed performance, not about the structural relations the student has acquired. The aim of the present pilot is to evaluate whether a brief computerised concept-mapping protocol can recover usable structural information at the level of an individual undergraduate cohort, as a precondition for deploying the same instrument at programme scale.

Sample & protocol

Cohort, instrument, five blocks

Sample. The cohort comprised twelve undergraduates of the BA Psychology programme at the Kyiv School of Economics, all enrolled in the third year of a four-year curriculum (April 2026; Nenrolled = 12; Ncomplete = 11). Recruitment was a full-cohort census of the third-year intake; participation was incentivised by the PSY555 course in which the protocol was administered.

The choice of programme stage and discipline was theoretically motivated rather than opportunistic. Third-year students at this institution have completed all core methods, biological-psychology, and social-psychology modules, placing them at a curriculum point where the relevant programme content is substantively familiar but has not yet been consolidated under thesis-stage selection pressure.

On the 14 PLOs

The 14 PLOs used in the protocol were not pulled from a single canonical document: there is no published programme-level list of that form at the institution. We derived the set ourselves by iteratively extracting and consolidating learning outcomes from individual course syllabuses across the three completed years of the curriculum, merging duplicates and resolving phrasing variants until the resulting list reproduced the substantive coverage of the programme's core. The 14-item set is therefore a research artefact, not an institutional one — a limitation explicitly recognised in §Limits.

 Full PLO definitions in the repo (lib/mockData.ts)

We treat the resulting sample as a convenience cohort with theoretical justification — adequate for pilot estimation of instrument behaviour, inadequate for population-level inference, and the intended baseline against which a larger pre-registered replication would be evaluated.

Instrument. Each session ran the same five-block sequence on the study app, in the order below. Full session traces are released in the public JSON. Per-step rationales draw on the cited literature reviewed above.

A · Self-rate

Likert competence rating per PLO

For each of the 14 PLOs the participant chooses a value on a 1–7 Likert scale ("Абсолютно не погоджуюсь" … "Повністю погоджуюсь") expressing agreement with "I can …" framings of the outcome.

Why first. Self-rating is collected before any mapping activity to capture declarative metacognition uncontaminated by the structural reflection that follows (Flavell, 1979). The same axis later supports the post-hoc test for illusory competence (Dunning & Kruger, 1999; Eva & Regehr, 2005).

B · Cluster

Group PLOs + name an anchor per group

Two-phase block. First the participant assigns each of the 14 PLOs to one of up to four colour-coded thematic groups. Then, for every used group, the participant selects a single anchor PLO — the concept that "best represents" that group.

Why. Expert categorisation is organised around deep principles, not surface features (Chi, Feltovich, & Glaser, 1981). Forced clustering elicits the participant's deepest categorical structure; the anchor selection probes whether the participant's representation contains hub concepts of the kind characteristic of scale-free networks (Barabási & Albert, 1999).

C · Map

Build a weighted concept graph

On a pannable canvas the participant draws an undirected edge between any two PLOs they perceive as related and assigns each edge a weight w ∈ {1, 2, 3} — weak, medium, strong association.

Why. Externalising the participant's representation as a weighted graph is the operationalisation of the cognitive-map hypothesis applied to conceptual space (Tolman, 1948; Constantinescu et al., 2016). Weighting forces an explicit cognitive-load trade-off (Sweller, 1988) and produces the graph-theoretic substrate on which density, WGD, and homophily are computed.

D · Pathfinding

Construct a route between two PLOs on your own map

Given a source and a target PLO, the participant builds a path by clicking intermediate concepts. The instruction is to "choose the route that feels most natural given your connections" — not a shortest-path computation.

Why. The capacity to lay out novel routes between known landmarks is the original behavioural test of a cognitive map (Tolman, 1948); the behavioural cost of the chosen route depends on global topology (Latora & Marchiori, 2001).

E · Perturbation

Pick the best replacement for a removed PLO

"The knowledge landscape has changed." One PLO from the participant's own graph is presented as struck through with its neighbours flagged. The participant chooses, from the remaining PLOs, the one that best fills the gap left by the removed concept (3–4 trials).

Why. Network robustness under targeted node removal is a property of topology (Barabási & Albert, 1999). The replacement task probes whether the participant's representation supports recovery of structural role from neighbourhood overlap — i.e. whether their map encodes the kind of expert chunking that survives a missing piece (Chase & Simon, 1973).

The app additionally collects basic demographic metadata between blocks C and D (block "Про вас" in the running app). It is not analysed in this report.

What we found

Three patterns GPA never sees

First curiosity

The cohort's three highest GPAs (96.0, 96.0, 94.0) built three of the most different graphs we saw. WGD = 0.751, "incomplete session", 0.729. GPA didn't predict map structure.

⚠ The ceiling came first

Nine of eleven students saturated density = 1.000. With near-zero variance, the four GPA-anchored probes on density, hierarchy, and weight differentiation aren't resolvable in this sample. That artefact is itself the first finding. Yet across those 9 saturated graphs mean weight ranges from 1.35 to 2.25 — a 1.7× spread on what looked like the same "everyone connects everything" pattern. Identical edge sets, very different conviction. Three further patterns emerged from the residual variance.

1. Higher map integration → lower self-rating (the Socratic effect)

ρ = −0.69, pperm = .023 Students whose maps look more integrated rate their own competence lower. The effect persists with the outlier removed (ρ = −0.58, N = 10) — the textbook Socratic pattern (Flavell, 1979; Eva & Regehr, 2005). By Cohen's (1988) heuristic, |ρ| = 0.69 is a large effect; the convention starts at 0.50.

2. More confident → slower under perturbation

ρ = +0.77, pperm = .008 Students with higher self-ratings take longer per perturbation choice, not shorter — most plausibly task investment. The single Dunning–Kruger case (next section) is off-trend in the opposite direction. By Cohen's heuristic this is a very large effect.

The ratio is dramatic

Fastest decider: 20.6 s per perturbation (S5). Slowest: 166.6 s (S11). That's an 8× spread — and the slowest students were the most confident. Confidence in this cohort buys deliberation, not speed.

3. All eleven students weight within-cluster ties higher

Without exception. 11 of 11 participants assigned a higher mean weight to edges within the four prior thematic clusters than to edges between them. Δ ranges from 0.20 (S6) to 0.90 (S5). Even the outlier with 17 edges shows Δ = 0.36. Across the cohort the within-cluster mean is 2.32; the between-cluster mean is 1.71 — without any prompting to differentiate by that criterion. The clustering structure of the curriculum is there in students' heads.

Why this matters at scale

Cluster homophily gives the next study a free ground truth. With 300+ participants and the same 14 PLOs, deviations from this 11/11 unanimity become diagnostic of confused or weakly-coupled segments of the programme — an automatic curriculum red-flag detector.

The single case

One participant. Nine indicators. A textbook profile.

One participant (S7, GPA = 93) deviates from the rest of the sample on nine indicators in a coherent direction — peak self-evaluation co-occurs with minimum structural competence. That's nine simultaneous deviations from the median in the predicted direction (Dunning & Kruger, 1999).

The cleanest signature

Across seven perturbation trials, S7 chose the most-strongly-connected neighbour of the removed node zero times. The group median: 67 %. Highest self-rating (6.71/7), flattest rating SD (0.47), worst structural accuracy (bridgeAcc = 0.12). Nine extremes, one direction.

Where this could go

This pilot answered one question: does the instrument produce a usable signal? It does. Three large effects in N = 11 — and a clear design fix for the ceiling. The natural next iteration would run the protocol on a much larger cohort with three changes that turn compliance back into information:

N ≈ 300+ Edge budget ≤ 30 Forced ranking Precision penalty Pre-registered

We can't yet promise when. If your programme would find a structural diagnostic useful, or if you want to run the same protocol on your cohort, write to us: dyashchuk@kse.org.ua · anovak@kse.org.ua.

Limitations

What this pilot can and cannot support

Every effect reported above must be read through the limitations below. They are ordered from most to least consequential and grouped by what they constrain — inference, sample, design, instrument, and operationalisation. None is individually fatal; jointly they are the reason this is framed as an exploratory pilot rather than a confirmatory study.

Statistical inference
L1Statistical power at N = 11

The asymptotic critical value of |ρ| for a two-tailed test of Spearman rank correlation at α = .05 is ≈ 0.62 (verified by 9 999-resample permutation in this study).

A medium effect by Cohen's (1988) heuristic (ρ ≈ .30) requires roughly N ≈ 80 to detect at 80% power; at N = 11 such effects are visible in scatterplots but indistinguishable from sampling noise. Three of the seven |ρ| ≥ 0.5 entries in the correlation matrix do not clear the asymptotic threshold despite their visible magnitude.

L2Multiple comparisons unadjusted

Eight pre-specified probes plus the full nine-by-nine correlation matrix produce a family of dozens of tests. Bonferroni-adjusted α at 36 unique pairs is ≈ .0014; under that threshold only WGD × avgWeight (p = .002) survives. Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control would pass two or three additional pairs depending on the chosen q.

We deliberately do not adjust in the main analysis because (a) the interpretive cost at N = 11 is high and (b) the inferential weight of any single p-value is already low. The implication for any downstream meta-analysis is that effect sizes, not p-values, are the export of this study.

L3Density-ceiling truncation of the predictor space

Var(density) ≈ 0.060 across the analytical sample, with 9 of 11 values exactly at 1.000, one at 0.835, and one at 0.187. Any test with density on either side reduces to a binary contrast {S7, S12} versus the rest — not a regression in any meaningful sense.

Four pre-specified probes (P2: GPA → density; P5: density → avgWeight; P7: edgeWeightSD → bridgeAcc; P8: zombie PLO → selfRating) are coded "not resolvable" rather than "null" for this reason. The fix in the next iteration is an explicit edge budget combined with a precision-weighted scoring rule.

L4Exploratory throughout

None of the eight directional probes constitutes a pre-registered hypothesis in the formal sense. The probes were specified before data inspection but were treated as exploratory throughout — in part because the operationalisations (which weighted-density formula, which Likert anchors, which perturbation scoring rule) were finalised in parallel with instrument development.

The Socratic effect, the confidence penalty, and the S7 single-case profile are post-hoc patterns identified after the density ceiling was observed. They should be read as hypothesis-generating for a pre-registered replication.

Sample & population
L5Single cohort, single programme, single institution

All eleven complete sessions come from one cohort of the BA Psychology programme at the Kyiv School of Economics. Generalisation to other institutions, programmes, year-stages, or cultural contexts is not warranted without replication. The compliance-adaptation finding in particular may be specific to a culture in which "covering everything" is a successful exam strategy.

KSE Psychology is a selective programme (cohort GPA range 82.11–96.00 in this sample). The relevance of a near-saturated GPA distribution to hypotheses about academic-success markers is itself a confound — see L8.

L6Convenience sample, not random

Recruitment was a full-cohort census of the third year, embedded in the PSY555 course in which the protocol was administered. Participation was thus effectively a course requirement rather than a freely chosen study sign-up; this raises self-selection concerns of a different sort than an opt-in sample, but does not eliminate them (motivation to engage seriously with the protocol still varied).

L7Cross-sectional, single snapshot

Each participant produced one map at one point in the academic year. Test–retest reliability of WGD, density, edge-weight SD, and self-rating is therefore unknown for this protocol. Within-subject change under instructional intervention or across the academic year cannot be assessed in this design.

A within-subject re-test after explicit instruction on edge-budget constraints is the natural next experiment — and would also bear directly on the compliance-adaptation interpretation.

L8No external criterion of subject knowledge

The design has no proctored test of psychology subject-matter competence. GPA proxies academic-system success — a function of grades, attendance, exam strategy, and institutional norms — not knowledge structure. Self-rating proxies metacognitive belief, not knowledge. Without an independent criterion we cannot distinguish "the participant has the structure but did not draw it" from "the participant does not have the structure".

Design & instrument
L9Pathfinding sub-sample collapse

The pathfinding block ran for only 2 of 11 complete sessions (S4, S9) due to a logged session-launcher misconfiguration that bypassed the block in nine sessions. P6 (edgeWeightSD → pathfindingAcc) is therefore reported as "not run" rather than null. Trial-level analysis on N = 2 yields ~6 trials and is statistically inert.

The fix is technical, not design-level: the next iteration ensures the block is mandatory for every session.

L10App-specific UX shapes the data

Edge creation runs on a pannable canvas with no explicit cap on edges and no friction beyond pointer cost. The interaction design itself privileges drawing-many-edges over drawing-careful-edges; the ceiling effect is partly an artefact of that privilege.

Categorical edge weights w ∈ {1, 2, 3} further under-discriminate — three levels are insufficient to recover meaningful hierarchical differentiation when edges saturate. A forced ranking of all drawn edges is the next-iteration replacement.

L11Self-rating wording primes positive bias

Each Likert item is framed in the affirmative ("Я можу …" / "I can …") with the seven anchors ranging from "Абсолютно не погоджуюсь" to "Повністю погоджуюсь". This is a standard agreement scale, but it primes acquiescence in a context where the probable response distribution is right-skewed by graduate-level academic self-presentation norms. The mean of 5.29 across the cohort and the heavy concentration at 5.71 are consistent with that bias.

Operationalisation
L12PLO list is researcher-derived

The 14-item PLO set was extracted iteratively by the authors from individual course syllabuses. There is no published programme-level document of that form at the institution. Phrasing choices, granularity decisions ("merge methods + design into one item or split"), and inclusion thresholds were resolved by author judgement.

Two consequences. First, the clustering block (block B) is partly a test of the authors' phrasing rather than the participants' representations: a participant who clusters by phrasing similarity will look as though they cluster by deep structure. Second, no inter-rater reliability statistic was computed for the PLO derivation itself — extraction was iterative and consensual, not blinded.

L13WGD construction is one of several plausible choices

Weighted graph density is computed as WGD = (Σ wij) / (3 · 91), normalising so that the complete graph with all weights equal to 3 has WGD = 1.0. The weight set {1, 2, 3} is treated as ordinal-and- approximately-interval; participants may use it ordinally only.

Alternative formulations (mean-weight × density, eigenvector centrality, spectral measures) would yield different rankings of the eleven graphs and potentially different Socratic-effect magnitudes. The choice of WGD is defensible but not unique.

L14Bridge-accuracy scoring is symmetric and unweighted

bridgeAcc = |N(removed) ∩ N(chosen)| / |N(removed)| rewards neighbourhood overlap but ignores edge weights and ignores higher-order structure (path length, betweenness, role similarity). A participant who chose a structurally analogous but distant node would score zero on this measure even if the choice is psychologically defensible.

L15S7 single-case is illustrative, not inferential

The Dunning–Kruger profile of S7 covers nine simultaneous deviations from the group median in the predicted direction. The combination is striking but is a single observation: it is consistent with the literature, not evidence for it. We report S7 as a profile worth describing in detail, not as a confirmed instance of a population-level effect.
