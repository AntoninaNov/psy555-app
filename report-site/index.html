Context and method

Twelve third-year students of the KSE “Psychology” programme (GPA: 82.11–96.00) completed a computerised protocol (11 finished in full): clustering of 14 intended learning outcomes (ILOs), construction of a weighted concept map (edge weights 1–3), self-rating of competence on each ILO (1–7 scale), a pathfinding task on the participant's own graph, and a perturbation task (choose a structural replacement for a removed node).

The study draws on Tinbergen's four-questions framework (1963) and the theory of evolutionary mismatch in education (Geary, 2008): the university system can incentivise cognitive accumulation of facts instead of building functional hierarchical knowledge networks.

The main unexpected finding: 9 of 11 participants built complete graphs (density = 1.0) — they connected all 91 possible ILO pairs. This ceiling effect made it impossible to test most pre-specified hypotheses, but is itself an important result.

Pre-specified hypotheses (H1–H6)

Six hypotheses were formulated before data collection on the basis of the evolutionary theoretical frame (Tinbergen, 1963; Geary, 2008). All correlations were computed with a Spearman permutation test (nresample = 9,999, seed = 42) on a sample of N = 11 (S2 excluded — incomplete session). At this N the critical |ρ| for α = .05 is ≈ 0.62 (two-tailed), so only large effects are detectable. The focus is on effect sizes and directions, not p-values.

H1

GPA → WGD · density · avgWeight
Hypothesis logic: top students draw more edges (H1b: density +) but each edge is weaker (H1c: avgWeight −). The two effects were expected to cancel out → overall integration WGD does not depend on GPA (H1a: ≈ 0). GPA is a marker of accumulation, not network quality.

What went wrong: H1c failed — top students set higher, not lower weights (ρ = +0.23 instead of −). The compensatory mechanism did not appear. So WGD correlates positively with GPA (+0.47) — the direct opposite of the «≈ 0» prediction. H1b is in the right direction, but the ceiling effect (density = 1.0 in most cases) prevents reliable testing.

In one sentence: H1 said «GPA and map quality are unrelated» — reality showed a moderate positive link, because the compensatory mechanism failed to materialise.

⚠ Key methodological finding: density ceiling effect

9 of 11 participants built complete graphs (density = 1.0) — they connected all 91 possible ILO pairs. All hypotheses H1–H5 rely on variation in density as either an independent or dependent variable. With near-zero variation in density, correlations with it cannot be statistically meaningful — so the hypotheses are untestable, not refuted. H6 is untestable by definition: at density = 1.0 no ILO is isolated (degree > 0 for all nodes). This is a compliance adaptation — students optimised their strategy to the absence of explicit constraints.

Hypothesis-by-hypothesis detail

H1 Evolutionary mismatch: GPA does not predict knowledge integration All three — ns

Theoretical rationale. The academic system rewards signalling of competence (GPA) — accumulation of facts — without requiring functional integration (Geary, 2008). Prediction: WGD (weighted density) correlates weakly with GPA, since the effect of more edges (H1b: density +) is offset by lower mean weights (H1c: avgWeight −).

H1a — GPA → WGD
ρ = +0.47 pperm = .153

Moderate positive effect, but below the critical threshold (|ρ| > 0.62). The direction is consistent with the ceiling effect: at density ≈ 1 in most participants, WGD essentially reflects only avgWeight, which correlates positively with GPA.

H1b — GPA → density
ρ = +0.30 pperm = .389

The predicted positive sign — a weak positive value is observed, but the variation in density is near zero in 9/11 participants. The test loses power because of a floor-floor contrast: density is either 1.0, or ~0.19–0.84.

H1c — GPA → avgWeight
ρ = +0.23 pperm = .497 Opposite sign

The hypothesis predicted a negative sign — a weak positive (ρ = +0.23) is observed. A possible explanation: students with higher GPA assign higher weights because they are more confident in their links — not because they draw fewer of them. This contradicts the trade-off version of the hypothesis but is consistent with an alternative: GPA correlates with a general tendency to make stronger statements.

H2 Evolutionary trade-off: number of links vs. their strength Opposite sign (ns)

Theoretical rationale. Cognitive resource is limited (Sweller, 1988; Kahneman, 2011). Maintaining more links simultaneously should «dilute» their subjective strength — hence the prediction of a negative correlation between density and mean weight.

ρ = +0.27 pperm = .431 — ns Expected −

A weak positive sign is observed, opposite to the prediction. Yet the effect is fully driven by a single observation (S7: density = 0.187, avgWeight = 1.24) against the rest (density ≈ 1.0, avgWeight = 1.35–2.25). With S7 excluded the relationship effectively disappears — i.e. H2 is untestable under the ceiling effect, not refuted. A real trade-off cannot be detected without genuine variation in density.

H3 «Flat» landscape of top students — absence of cognitive hierarchy Null (ns)

Theoretical rationale. Natural navigation systems hierarchise routes by frequency and salience (O'Keefe & Nadel, 1978). The university system encourages an even-handed approach to all topics — «everything is important». Prediction: top students build flatter maps with lower weight variance (SD → less hierarchy).

ρ = +0.11 pperm = .753 — ns Expected −

An effectively null effect with a slight positive deviation — opposite to the prediction. Weight variance for most students has shifted into a relatively standard range (SD ≈ 0.58–0.83), which makes it impossible to distinguish «flat» and «hierarchical» builders under complete graphs. H3 needs a design that forces edge hierarchisation for proper testing.

H4 Navigational extrapolation: hierarchical maps → better pathfinding N = 2 — not tested

Theoretical rationale. Effective navigation requires the ability to lay out new routes between known landmarks (Tolman, 1948; Chase & Simon, 1973). Students with hierarchical maps (high SD of weights) would be expected to find more optimal routes than those with equal-weight edges.

The pathfinding task was completed by only two participants due to technical conditions of the protocol launch. With N = 2 any correlation estimate is statistically meaningless. H4 is fully untestable in the data. Recommendation for future studies: include the task as mandatory for all participants.

H5 Landscape robustness: hierarchical maps tolerate node loss Null (ns)

Theoretical rationale. Hierarchical networks are more robust to targeted attacks on nodes thanks to alternative routes (Barabási & Albert, 1999). Bridge accuracy measures how structurally relevant the chosen replacement for a removed node is — i.e. the functional robustness of the map. Formula: bridgeAcc = |neighbors(removed) ∩ neighbors(chosen)| / |neighbors(removed)|.

ρ = −0.03 pperm = .925 — ns

An effectively null correlation. Variation in edgeWeightSD under complete graphs is limited (SD ranges 0.58–0.83 for 9/11 participants), which lowers test power. A larger lever for studying H5: use trial-level data (trial-level analysis), giving N ≈ 3–4 trials × 11 students = ~33–44 observations.

H6 Illusory competence: zombie ILO → inflated self-rating Structurally untestable

Theoretical rationale. A student who has learned a concept in isolation (for an exam) has declarative knowledge without procedural integration. Self-rating relies on declarative knowledge («I know what this is»), not on network position («I can use this in the context of other ideas») — hence illusory competence (Flavell, 1979; Dunning & Kruger, 1999).

Predicted test: compare selfRating for nodes with degree = 0 («zombie ILOs») vs. degree > 0 (Wilcoxon signed-rank per student + binomial test across the group). Result: untestable. At density = 1.0 all 14 ILOs have degree > 0 for 9/11 participants — no zombie node in the data. H6 can only be tested with a design that explicitly caps the number of allowed edges (for example, ≤ 30).

Exploratory finding 1: Socratic effect

The most unexpected result of the study was a negative correlation between weighted graph density (WGD) and mean self-rated competence across ILOs. Students with more integrated, «heavier» concept maps rated their own competence lower ρ = −0.69, pperm = .023*.

Excluding S7 (outlier): ρ = −0.59 — the effect remains moderate even without the extreme observation. This reproduces the «Socratic effect»: deeper understanding of links between concepts comes with greater awareness of one's own knowledge gaps (Flavell, 1979; Eva & Regehr, 2005).

Student S6 (GPA = 89, WGD = 0.740) — lowest mean self-rating (4.71). Student S7 (GPA = 93, WGD = 0.077) — highest self-rating (6.71) with the worst bridge accuracy (0.12).

Exploratory finding 2: Confidence penalty

Students with higher mean self-rating spent more time on the perturbation task (choosing a structural replacement for a removed ILO): ρ = +0.77, pperm = .008**. This result contradicts the intuitive expectation that more confident students solve the task faster.

A possible explanation: students with higher self-rating take the task more seriously and analyse alternatives more carefully. The exception is S7 (avgRating = 6.71): despite the highest self-rating, his time was relatively short (114.8 s), but bridge accuracy was the worst (0.12) — «fast and wrong».

Exploratory finding 3: Cluster homophily

All eleven participants without exception assigned higher weights to edges within thematic clusters than to edges between clusters (Δ = 0.20 to 0.90). This pattern is unusually robust — even S7, who drew only 17 edges, shows homophily = 0.36.

Cluster homophily confirms that the prior thematic clustering of the 14 ILOs into 4 groups reflects something semantically real in students' perception. Within-cluster links subjectively feel stronger, even without an explicit instruction to differentiate weights along that criterion.

The exception is S6 (Δ = 0.20): the most «cross-cluster» mind — possibly a more integrative, cross-disciplinary view of the programme.

Exploratory finding 5: Correlation matrix

The full Spearman rank-correlation matrix (N = 11) reveals several additional stable links. The most important (|ρ| > 0.5):

  • ρ(WGD, avgWeight) = +0.86, pperm = .002** — more integrated maps have stronger mean weights
  • ρ(density, bridgeAcc) = +0.68, pperm = .020* — higher graph density → better bridge accuracy
  • ρ(WGD, avgRating) = −0.69, pperm = .023* — Socratic effect (Fig. 1)
  • ρ(avgRating, perturbTime) = +0.77, pperm = .008** — confidence penalty (Fig. 2)
  • ρ(density, avgRating) = −0.61, pperm = .044* — incomplete graphs are associated with higher self-rating

Key findings

Complete-graph phenomenon: compliance adaptation as a strategy. 9 of 11 students connected all 91 ILO pairs (density = 1.0). This evolutionarily suboptimal behaviour is interpreted as compliance adaptation: in the absence of a clear «wrong», the student minimises the risk of missing the «right».
Socratic effect (ρ = −0.69, pperm = .023*): stronger integration of the concept map is associated with lower self-rated competence. Students who see more links between concepts are better aware of the limits of their knowledge.
Confidence penalty (ρ = +0.77, pperm = .008**): higher self-rating → longer perturbation decision time. Contradicts the «more confident = faster» expectation; may reflect more careful analysis of alternatives.
Dunning–Kruger profile (S7): the only participant with a very incomplete graph (density = 0.19) shows the combination of maximum self-rating (6.71/7), fastest decisions (10.5 s/edge), and lowest bridge accuracy (0.12). «Fast, confident, inaccurate».
Cluster homophily — a stable structure: all 11 participants without exception assign higher weights to within-cluster links (Δ = 0.20–0.90). Confirms the semantic validity of the thematic clustering of ILOs.

Implications and limitations

This pilot study uncovered an unexpected phenomenon: when building concept maps of intended learning outcomes, most students resorted to a strategy of maximum coverage. This behaviour — compliance adaptation — is a serious methodological challenge for instruments of this type and at the same time an important standalone result: it shows that academic culture can shape cognitive strategies independently of subject knowledge.

Practical implication: concept-mapping instruments need design choices that make a full-coverage strategy impossible or unrewarding (for example, capping the number of edges, or ranking instead of binary choice). Without these they will measure compliance, not the structure of knowledge.


⚠ Key limitations

  • N = 11 (out of 12 enrolled) — no correlation reaches significance after FDR correction; all results are preliminary
  • Density ceiling effect — 9/11 complete graphs made testing of H1–H5 impossible
  • Pathfinding N = 2 — the pathfinding task was completed by only 2 participants
  • H6 untested — complete graphs have no zombie nodes (degree = 0)
  • S2 — incomplete session (0 edges); excluded from all graph analyses
  • Cross-sectional design — no repeated measurements
  • No external knowledge criterion — GPA as a proxy, no independent test of competence
